Write Up

What we measure

NDVI is a satellite index of vegetation greenness. The year views show the seasonal value. The change view subtracts the drought year’s NDVI from the wetter year’s NDVI (or comparison minus reference for custom years). Greenness also responds to factors such as irrigation, land use, and fire; the comparisons do not isolate the effect of drought.

How the sample was built

The 12 California GeoTIFFs were exported from NASA Terra MODIS MOD13A2.061. The export averaged good-quality observations with composite start dates in April–September, then aggregated to 5 km California Albers cells. We retained 16,933 cells with valid NDVI in all 12 years. Missing or fill values were excluded; valid negative values were kept.

Rationale

Our main goal for this visualization was to show how vegetation in California changed over time during periods of drought and wetter years. In order to display this information, we considered many different marks and visualizations to best present our vegetation data. Namely, we used area maps and line marks. The pros for using a line mark visual is that it is easy to identify clear shifts/differences in vegetation over time however it does not capture the data for specific areas and the pros for an area map visualization is that it is easy to read and see data for specific parts of California but it is difficult to see clear differences as well as the magnitude of such differences. For these reasons, in our final visual, we decided to use both types; we used an area map of California using color and position to encode data for our main graph while using a line graph to see a more accurate change in vegetation for any given point on the map. For our map, we choose to use an interactive slider because it allows readers to easily see the difference in vegetation for different years by comparing the colors of the two years. Using colors as the main channel to convey information is effective because it shows the vegetation levels of each area in an easily digestible way for readers. Because of the cons of a map visualization, we decided to include an interactive line graph where readers can click any point on the map and a line visualization that shows the change in NDVI compared to California’s mean NDVI over time will appear. This ensured that we accounted for the pros and the cons for both types of visuals while creating a fun interactive design. Additionally, in order to highlight the stark differences between drought periods and wetter years, we decided to add two main timeframes for comparison, 2014-2017 and 2021-2023. These years clearly demonstrate the change when California was in a drought (2014, 2021) to wetter years (2017, 2023). We did this to make it easier for the reader to see the effects of a drought instead of having to randomly pick years to compare, since comparing two drought years or two wet years would not provide a lot of useful information. We also added an interactive zoom feature. We added this because it makes it easier for readers to choose a specific area to look at instead of the whole of California. Finally, we added a map that shows the change in NDVI for two years with green being an increase, orange being a decrease, and gray being small/no change. We added this noninteractive feature to clearly underline which areas gained/lost vegetation over time and to show this change over all of California. An alternative interactive technique we considered was a different type of slider that had two years in the same map and the slider would show one year on one side and another year on the other. We ultimately chose not to use this technique because it was more difficult to see gradual changes over time since it would only allow for the comparison of two years at a time and we wanted to emphasize the effect that droughts had on California vegetation over a period of time.

Overview of Development

Our team consisted of Justin Quesada, Will Shin, and Justin Shiu. We divided the work evenly, with each member responsible for a major component of the application and contributing to testing and revisions.

Justin Shiu focused on data preparation and validation, organizing the MODIS NDVI values across years and checking that comparisons used consistent locations and seasonal coverage. He also developed the data notes explaining how the sample was constructed.

Will Shin focused on the interactive California map, including the year controls, greenness and change views, zooming, and cell selection. He worked on making changes between drought and wetter years easy to explore.

Justin Quesada focused on the trend chart, summary statistics, and overall page design. He connected the supporting charts to the map selection and styled the layout, legends, and controls to keep the interface consistent.

We estimate that we spent approximately 20 people-hours on the application, with each member contributing about 7 hours. Data preparation, map interactions, and coordinating updates across the visualizations took the most time. We worked iteratively, beginning with a basic map and then adding comparisons, supporting charts, and interface improvements. All three members shared responsibility for testing the controls, checking displayed values, refining the colors, and revising the explanatory text.
